How Temp Alert works

What it does

Temp Alert watches your temperature sensors and emails people when one goes too cold or too hot — a frost warning from a greenhouse bench, a heat warning from a propagation house. Each sensor can have its own alerts, each with its own temperature and its own people.

It runs inside a Google Sheet that you own. A small script in the sheet checks every 15 minutes on Google’s computers, so it keeps watching when your phone and computer are off. This app is how you set it up and how the crew sees the readings.

How the alerts behave

  • One email when a reading crosses the line (“at or below 32 °F”), not one every 15 minutes while it stays there.
  • One email when it is back to normal — a couple of degrees past the line (the margin, 2 °F unless you change it), so a reading hovering right at 32 does not send twenty emails.
  • One email if a sensor stops reporting for more than two hours (you can change that), and one when it is back. A dead sensor must never look like a quiet night.
  • Every email is written on the sheet’s Alert log tab. The alerts themselves are on the Alerts tab — one row per alert, which you can also edit by hand.

Where the readings come from

  • LI-COR — your own sensors, through your LI-COR Cloud token. Usually a new reading every few minutes. The token is kept inside the sheet’s script: not on any phone, not in the sheet.
  • NEWA stations (Cornell’s Network for Environment and Weather Applications) — farm weather stations across the Northeast. Hourly; the newest hour can take a while to appear.
  • Weather Service stations (mostly airports) — hourly, sometimes more often.

A station miles away is not your coldest bench — frost settles in low spots and under clear skies. Your own sensor where the plants are is always the better trigger.

📱 Make sure an alert wakes you up

Alerts are ordinary emails from the Google account that owns the sheet, and every subject starts with [Temp Alert]. A phone only rings for email if it is told to. Each person on the alerts should do these steps once.

  1. Never in spam, always important (Gmail)
    • On a computer, in Gmail, type subject:"[Temp Alert]" in the search box and press Enter.
    • Click the “Show search options” sliders at the right of the search box → Create filter.
    • Tick “Never send it to Spam” and “Always mark it as important”, then Create filter.
    • Not on Gmail? Add the sending address to your contacts so it is never treated as spam.
  2. Let the phone notify you
    • Gmail app (iPhone or Android): ☰ menu → Settings → your account → Notifications → “High priority only” (or “All”). The filter above makes every alert high priority.
    • iPhone Mail app: open a test alert, tap the sender, “Add to VIP”. Then Settings → Notifications → Mail → turn on alerts and a sound for VIP.
    • Outlook app: open a test alert → ⋯ → Move to Focused → Always move to Focused, and make sure Outlook notifies for Focused mail.
  3. Let it through at night
    • iPhone: Settings → Focus → Sleep (and Do Not Disturb) → Apps → add Gmail or Mail to the allowed apps.
    • Android: Settings → Notifications → Do Not Disturb → Apps → add Gmail.
  4. Test it the way it will happen
    • Send a test alert (above), lock your phone, turn on Sleep / Do Not Disturb — and check that it rings. Menus differ a little between phones and app versions; the test is the real check.

Email is good for a heads-up. For something that must wake someone every time — heaters, frost protection — keep a second way too (a phone call tree, a hard-wired alarm). Google checks every 15 minutes, and email can be delayed.

Limits worth knowing

  • Google lets a free Gmail account send to about 100 recipients a day (Google Workspace: 1,500). One alert to three people counts as three. Plenty for alerts — but a test sent over and over uses it up. Setup shows what is left today.
  • The check runs every 15 minutes, and email can take a minute or two more. Use it for warnings, not as the only safeguard for anything that cannot wait.
  • The emails come from the Google account that owns the sheet. A shared farm account is better than someone’s personal one.

Who can see what

Anyone with the share link sees the readings and which alerts are on. Only someone with the setup password sees the email addresses, changes the alerts or the LI-COR token, and turns checking on or off.
